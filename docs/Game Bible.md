# Strange Ranger: Game Bible

Nov 14, 2026 · @Mark

This is the master record of what Strange Ranger is, for the whole game. It says what to build and why, never how; each phase gets its own build spec in the other tabs.

## What Strange Ranger is

Strange Ranger is a cozy, top-down pixel art game about a new park ranger who explores, opens and runs a vast, quietly magical wilderness park. It joins the wide, weather-ruled wilderness of The Long Dark to the gentle, do-what-you-like days of Stardew Valley.

The player wakes each morning and decides what the day is for. They might hike out to map new ground, fix a cabin, tidy the park, befriend something odd in the woods, or drink coffee in town. Weather, season and distance nudge the plan. Nothing ever orders the player around.

The game rests on five pillars:

- **Your day, your whim.** Every activity is offered and none is required.
- **A big place that takes time to know.** Distance is real. Trips need a plan, supplies and somewhere to sleep.
- **Exploring opens the park.** Nothing becomes a tour, a campsite or an income until the ranger has found it, mapped it and made it ready.
- **Forgiving, always.** The worst outcome is lost time, a little lost money, a grumpy note, or mischief. The game never ends in failure.
- **Weird, cozy and unexplained.** The park is full of magic that nobody finds remarkable, and it grows stranger the further in you go.

## Standing rules

Every feature, creature and event must pass these rules. When a design question is not answered anywhere else, these answer it.

1. **When in doubt, do it like Stardew Valley.** Any mechanic this bible does not describe works the way Stardew's does: saving on sleep, inventory, tool bar, dialogue, friendship, shops, festivals, menus.
2. **Everything is optional.** No system may force the player onto a track. Invitations are fine; assignments are not.
3. **Nothing fails the game.** Neglect costs opportunity or invites mischief. It never ends a save or locks content for good.
4. **Missed things come back.** Events repeat next month or next year.
5. **No killing.** The ranger carries no weapon and nothing dies, including on screen by other hands.
6. **No reflex tests.** Minigames are welcome if they are relaxed or thoughtful. Nothing depends on fast fingers.
7. **Nothing sinister.** Mysteries are curious and warm. There is no real peril, horror or villain.
8. **Magic is never explained.** Anything may exist in the park. The weirder, cozier and zanier, the better.
9. **Locals find it all ordinary.** Townsfolk treat the strangest things as everyday life.
10. **Exploring is the upgrade path.** The best gear, recipes and opportunities come from things found, not things bought.
11. **One view, always.** The game is top-down at all times. No cut-ins, panoramas or changes of camera.
12. **The same park for everyone.** The land is identical in every save, so players can learn it and compare notes.

## The ranger, the Chief and the story

The player is a new ranger, alone, sent to open up and run an old park whose last ranger is unaccounted for.

**The ranger.** Character creation is light, like Stardew's: name, gender, a few appearance choices, and a pet. There is no class or backstory to pick.

**The Chief Ranger.** He is an older man, warm and supportive, who is always reachable and never comes to the park. He speaks through radio calls, notes and the yearly review. He sends word when a region opens, a gentle grumble when bathrooms and trash are neglected, and praise when things go well. He is plainly holding something back, and why he never visits is one of the long mysteries.

**The last ranger.** Nobody is sure what became of them. Their trail runs through the whole game, mostly as journal pages left in cabins. The tone is curious, never grim.

**Mysteries.** Each region holds two or three mysteries of its own, so they arrive at a gentle pace. A few long threads, the last ranger and the Chief among them, run across all five years. All of them are optional.

**The long arc.** Three soft measures give players something to aim at:

- A gentle **yearly review** from the Chief, which can be retried.
- A **park status** that rises as the park grows, from forgotten backwater to famous destination.
- The **last ranger's story**, for those who follow it.

The game continues without end after all three.

## The park

The park has five large regions, each about one in-game day's walk across, so the whole park is roughly a five-day walk end to end.

**Regions.** The first is the dark forest, which holds the park entrance, the ranger station and Skull Lake. The others are the swamp, the plains, the foothills and the mountains of snow and ice. The Dusk River runs through all five and ties them together. Each region has its own palette, creatures, weather and cast of oddities.

**How regions open.** One new region opens each year, at the start of spring, announced by the Chief. The calendar opens the gate and the player decides whether to walk through it. A player still busy in the forest loses nothing by staying. Until its year comes, a region cannot be entered.

**A year of play.** A year is four seasons of 28 days, 112 days in all. At Stardew's pace that is about 26 hours of play. Each region must hold enough to fill its year without the player feeling they are waiting. The full park takes five years, well over 100 hours.

**Scale.** Unlike Stardew, the map cannot be crossed quickly. Near sites are day trips. Far sites are overnight trips. The far mountains are a week's expedition unless shortcuts have been opened.

**Shelter.** Cabins and fire towers are plentiful. There is always one within reach, so distance creates planning and never danger.

**A fixed land.** The terrain, landmarks, sites, mysteries and story are the same in every save and for every player. Knowing the park is a skill that takes many hours to earn.

**What shifts between saves.** Forage spots, supplies found in cabins and the whereabouts of creatures move a little each game. Weather is always unpredictable. Experience helps, but the player still has to look.

## Days, seasons, weather and night

Time runs at Stardew's pace: a day lasts about 14 real minutes, and the player sleeps to end it and save.

**The day.** There is never enough day for everything, by design. Staying out late for the night's magic means a slow morning after. Staying up far too late ends in a collapse and a rescue.

**Seasons change the land.** Each season alters where the ranger can go, who is out there and how busy the park is. A single region offers four different versions of itself in a year. Examples from the forest:

- Skull Lake freezes in winter, so its islands can be reached on foot.
- Some trails snow in and close.
- Certain creatures appear only in one season.
- Summer brings the big crowds. Winter is quiet and wild.

**Weather changes plans.** Rain, fog, storms, snow and blizzards all exist, and they matter. A blizzard at the door is a reason to rethink the day. Fog and snow hide the trail and make getting lost likely.

**The forecast.** A radio at the ranger station gives a morning forecast that is usually right. It is less reliable for distant regions. Fire towers have radios too. Away from a radio, the ranger reads the sky: clouds building, wind rising.

**Night.** Night is where the magic gathers. Creatures, glowing places and paths appear that do not exist by day, and the further the park has been explored, the wilder nights become. Night also makes getting lost easier, and it costs energy.

**Aurora nights.** These are rare nights when the whole park goes strange. They are never on the radio forecast. The fortune teller in town is the one who hints that one is coming.

**Calendar events.** Some puzzles and happenings occur only on a certain day of a month or season, such as the first full moon of autumn. Players can mark the date and plan a trip around it. Missing one means waiting for it to come round again.

## Survival and getting around

The ranger manages two needs, energy and warmth, and running out of either ends in a rescue, never a death.

**Energy.** Work and travel spend it. Food and sleep restore it. It works like Stardew's energy bar.

**Warmth.** Cold weather, wind, rain and night drain it. Clothing slows the loss. Fires and shelter restore it. Warmth is what makes weather change plans.

**What is left out.** There is no thirst, no separate hunger and no carry weight.

**The backpack.** It holds a limited number of items, by slots. It can be upgraded. Trips need supplies: food, warm clothes, repair materials. Supplies are plentiful and easy to come by.

**Collapse and rescue.** If energy or warmth runs out, or the ranger stays up far too late, they collapse. They wake at the station or the nearest repaired cabin, having lost some time and a little money. This is the game's only penalty for pushing too hard.

**Getting lost.** Trails are the safe way through. Off the trail, especially at night or in fog or snow, the ranger can lose their way. Being lost costs time and may end in a rescue. It is never worse than that.

**Cabins and fire towers.** These are places to rest, warm up, sleep and save. They start run-down and must be repaired before they can be used. Once repaired they can be furnished and stocked.

**Faster travel, earned over time:**

- A **ranger utility cart**, a small four-wheeled truck. It runs only on trails that have been upgraded to cart width, so trail work pays off as speed.
- A **canoe or raft** on the Dusk River, which carries the ranger and supplies quickly downstream.
- **Something magical**, to be found and never bought.

The deep backcountry stays on foot.

## Mapping

The map starts blank and fills in through four actions, so a casual player ends with a usable but patchy map and a thorough one fills the page.

| Action | What it reveals | Needs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Walking | A thin trace of where the ranger has been | Nothing |
| Surveying | A patch around the ranger | Map, compass and pencil; a little time; clear conditions |
| Mapping a location | The site and some surrounding ground | Finding the site |
| Surveying from a fire tower or scenic outlook | A much larger patch | Reaching the tower or outlook |

**Surveying takes time.** It is a deliberate pause. It cannot be done in fog, falling snow or darkness.

**Where am I?** The ranger's position shows only on ground that has been mapped. It fades out in fog, in storms, and at night away from a trail. On unmapped ground the player sees the trace behind them and nothing ahead.

**Completing a region.** When about 80% of a region is mapped, the rest fills in and the region's map is complete. The threshold is below 100% so nobody hunts for one missing sliver.

**The reward feeds the park.** A completed map becomes the official park map. The gift shop sells copies, visitors get lost less often, and the Chief sends a rare pleased note. There may also be a special reward for completion.

**Skull Lake.** The lake's skull shape cannot be seen from the ground. It appears only as the map fills in.

## Opening sites and running the park

Nothing in the park earns money or welcomes visitors until the ranger has opened it, and opening a site is a small project with several steps.

**Opening a site.** The steps, in rough order:

1. Find it.
2. Map it.
3. Clear the trail to it, or build one.
4. Repair what is there.
5. Put up signs.
6. Settle whatever magic is going on.

None of this is automatic. Each site gives a reason to return several times.

**Once open, the park runs it.** The ranger does not lead the tours or staff the desk. An open site produces tours, camping permits, rentals and entrance fees by itself. A player who never explores simply earns nothing from tours.

**Income.** Money comes from entrance fees, guided tours, camping permits, rentals, fishing permits, the gift shop, and things the ranger makes and sells.

**Building.** Large facilities, such as campgrounds, bathrooms and docks, go at set places that the ranger earns and builds. Small things, such as benches, signs and trash cans, can be placed freely anywhere open. Every player's park ends up looking like their own.

**Upkeep and neglect.** Bathrooms need cleaning and trash needs collecting. Neglect brings a grumbling note from the Chief and more chaos-causing troll badgers. It never brings failure. A suggestion box collects what visitors think.

**Life gets easier.** Earnings buy staff who take over cleaning and trash, better gear and clothing, cabin and tower upgrades, faster travel, and improvements to the park. Chores fade over the years, as they do in Stardew.

**Visitors.** Visitors are small characters in the world. They walk the trails, camp, queue at bathrooms, get lost in fog and need finding, and are delighted or spooked by what they meet. They can only go where the ranger has opened. The crowd spreads visibly across the map as the park grows, and the deep wilderness stays empty. Scout troops are one kind of visitor, with their own needs and comedy.

**Who knows about the magic.** Most visitors think this is an ordinary park. A few kinds, such as cryptid hunters and scouts, are looking hard. For each magical site, the ranger chooses whether to keep it secret, hint at it, or put it on the brochure. Going public brings money and attention. Keeping it private keeps the creature content and its friendship growing. Neither is wrong, so one player's park is a quiet reserve with secrets and another's is the weirdest attraction in the country.

**Fishing.** The ranger never fishes. Visitors do, catch and release, with permits and rented rods. The ranger instead spots and photographs what lives in the water for the field guide. The ranger can also set a line or net and collect it later; it brings up objects and clues, never fish.

**Water.** The lake offers boat rentals, swimming in summer and skating in winter. The river offers float trips.

## Creatures and magic

The park is home to ordinary animals and to magical ones, and every magical one is a small problem with its own kind of answer, none of which is violence.

**Anything goes.** Folklore, cryptids, fairy folk, flying saucers and giant robots all share the park. Each region has its own cast: Bigfoot in the forest, a yeti in the mountains. Nothing is explained.

**Strange cousins.** Many ordinary animals have a magical counterpart: badgers and troll badgers, deer and magic deer, moose and a were-moose. An ordinary animal is a hint that something odder is near. The field guide has two halves to match.

**Each creature has its own answer.** Dealing with a magical thing might mean any of these, and different creatures call for different ones:

- Befriending it with gifts, as with Stardew's villagers.
- Working out what it wants.
- Striking a deal.
- Coaxing it to move somewhere else.

**Answers cross regions.** The thing one creature wants is often found far away: mushrooms from the swamp for Bigfoot in the forest. Old regions stay useful after they are opened, and any odd find might solve a problem elsewhere.

**After it is settled.** A creature may leave or may stay and become part of the park. Where it stays, the ranger chooses whether the public ever learns of it.

**The fortune teller.** She gives hints about creatures, cryptids and coming magical nights. She is the game's gentle hint system, in place of quest markers.

**Giant robots and a Monument Valley place.** A later region holds huge ancient robots standing in the landscape like rock formations. What they are stays open.

**Hidden things.** In every region the lab invents secrets of its own, within the standing rules, and keeps them out of any document the designer reads. The designer should be surprised by their own game.

## Tools, crafting, skills and badges

Nearly everything the ranger does goes through a small kit of tools, each of which can be improved with magic found in the park.

**The kit.** There is no weapon of any kind.

| Tool | Used for |
| --- | --- |
| Axe or saw | Clearing fallen trees and brush |
| Shovel | Building and widening trail |
| Hammer | Repairs and construction |
| Lantern | Light at night and in caves |
| Map, compass and pencil | Surveying and finding the way |
| Camera and binoculars | Recording creatures and landmarks for the field guide |

**Three kinds of crafting:**

- **Park work:** signs, bridges, trail markers, cabin repairs.
- **Personal:** gear, clothing, food for the trail.
- **Goods to sell:** gift shop items made from odd finds, such as carved cryptid figures or jars of glowing moss.

**Materials.** Basic supplies are bought in town or gathered. Special and magical ingredients must be found.

**The magic slot.** Every recipe has a basic version anyone can make, plus an optional slot for a found magical ingredient that changes or improves the result. A lantern made with a magic crystal might reveal hidden paths. Boots made with something from the swamp might cross mud. The best gear cannot be bought.

**Clothing.** Clothes are for looks and for warmth. They are bought at the outfitter or crafted, with magical versions through the magic slot.

**Skills.** Five skills rise by doing the activity, each offering a choice of specialty along the way, as in Stardew.

| Skill | Grows by |
| --- | --- |
| Trailcraft | Clearing, building and signing trails |
| Surveying | Mapping, navigating, finding the way in bad weather |
| Woodcraft | Repairs, crafting and camp life, including fires and cooking |
| Foraging | Gathering ordinary and magical ingredients |
| Lore | Understanding and befriending creatures |

Running the park sits outside the skill system for now.

**Merit badges.** The ranger earns badges for achievements, such as mapping, trail building, creature friendships and night photography. Badges are the game's achievement system. They also quietly show the player what is possible without telling them to do it.

**Minigames.** Surveying, repairs and creature encounters may use minigames, provided they are relaxed or thoughtful.

## Humdrum, home and pets

The town of Humdrum sits right outside the park gate, a few minutes' walk from the ranger station, so coffee, supplies and gossip are close on days spent near home. The name is provisional.

**The town.** Humdrum has an outfitter, a coffee shop, a Mexican restaurant, a library, a gift shop and a fortune teller. Its people think nothing ever happens there.

**The townsfolk.** They are full characters in the Stardew manner. Each has a daily routine, a friendship that grows through talk and gifts, a personal story that unfolds over time, and optional quests. Romance works as it does in Stardew. All of it can be ignored.

**Distance has a cost.** A ranger three days out at Skull Lake is far from town and will miss things, such as a birthday or a festival. Heading out is a real choice.

**Festivals.** Humdrum holds festivals on set dates each season. The park hosts more of its own as it grows.

**The town grows.** As the park's status rises over the years, new shops open and new people move in.

**The ranger station.** This is the ranger's home at the park entrance. It starts small and shabby, like Stardew's farmhouse. It can be upgraded and decorated with furniture and with trophies from finds. It has a small garden out the back.

**Backcountry homes.** Every cabin and fire tower can be repaired, furnished and stocked. A favorite cabin by Skull Lake can become a second home, and players build their own chain of cozy stops across the park.

**Pets.** The player chooses one pet at the start. The two kinds help in different ways:

| Pet | Character | How it helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dog | The practical trail partner | Sniffs out hidden things, leads the ranger back to a path when lost, helps find lost visitors |
| Cat | The magical one | Senses hidden magic, is at its best at night, turns up at cabins by itself, leads to secrets, makes shy creatures trusting, guards the garden from troll badgers, leaves small finds on the doorstep |

The choice leans a game toward the ranger side or the mystery side without closing anything off. Later in the game the player can adopt the other.

## Year one: the dark forest and Skull Lake

The first version of the game is the entrance area, the dark forest, Skull Lake and Humdrum. It is a full year of play, about 26 hours, and must feel complete by itself.

**Skull Lake.** The lake is shaped like a giant skull, with islands forming its features. The islands are reached by boat in summer and on foot across the ice in winter.

**Places.** Twelve sites are agreed. Each is found, mapped and opened as described above.

| Site | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| The Hollow Giant | A tree so large a past ranger built a cabin inside its trunk. Likely the first backcountry home. |
| Static Tower | The first fire tower. Its radio picks up a station that does not exist, which plays tomorrow's forecast. |
| The Sprite Market | A mushroom ring in a meadow, empty by day. At night sprites set up stalls and trade odd goods for odd things. |
| Troll Badger Bridge | The only crossing on a stretch of the Dusk River. The badgers charge a toll in snacks or shiny trash. |
| Raccoon Camp | An abandoned campground where raccoons live in the tents and run it like a small town. They need convincing to share. |
| Gord's Meadow | Home of Gord, a moose who becomes a rather awkward man at the full moon and wants help fitting in at the coffee shop. |
| The Teeth | A curved row of standing stones on the south shore, forming the skull's teeth. They hum on aurora nights. |
| Left Eye Island | A hot spring that never freezes. Bears use it as a spa and have firm views about sharing. |
| Right Eye Island | A small lighthouse nobody remembers building. Its light is on every night. |
| Nose Island | A cave mouth leading down under the lake. The deepest, strangest site in the region, for late in the year. |
| Upfalls | A waterfall that runs normally by day and flows upward after dark, giving access to a ledge. |
| The Elf Post Office | Tiny doors at the base of an old tree. Once the elves trust the ranger, they carry items between cabins. |

**Mysteries.**

- **The last ranger.** Journal pages are scattered through the cabins. They read as cheerful and increasingly distracted. The final page points toward Nose Island.
- **The lighthouse keeper.** Who lights the lamp on Right Eye Island, and what is it signaling to?
- **The wandering signs.** Trail signs the ranger puts up point somewhere else by morning. The badgers are the obvious suspects, and they did not do it.

**Residents.** Ordinary: bears, squirrels, rabbits, badgers, raccoons, deer, moose, hedgehogs. Magical: jackalopes, troll badgers, magic deer, Gord the were-moose, Bigfoot, sprites, elves, fairies and nymphs.

**Hidden things.** The lab adds secrets of its own to the forest, as described under Creatures and magic.

## Look, platform and how the game is built

Strange Ranger looks like a cozy 16-bit top-down pixel art game, set in a wilderness park, and is a solo PC game played with keyboard and mouse.

**The look.** Tiles are 16 by 16 pixels, and the ranger is one tile wide and two tall. Sunsets, sunrises, storms and the aurora are shown by light and color washing over the world. The sense of distance comes from how long things take to reach. The full art rules are in the separate art style guide.

**Platform.** PC first, keyboard and mouse, as Stardew began. No multiplayer is planned.

**How it is built.** The game is built in Godot by an autonomous lab, in phases. Each phase adds one layer on top of tested bedrock and can be played by itself.

**Content lives in data.** Every item, recipe, creature, townsperson, site and event is a row in a content table. The code reads the tables. Adding a creature means adding rows, not writing code.

**The land is built once.** Wilderness may be generated by rules to save effort, but it is generated once and frozen, so every player gets the same park.

**Art.** Pixel art is made outside the lab from prompts in the art style guide, delivered phase by phase. The lab builds with placeholders under final file names, and real art drops in over them.

**This document.** The bible changes only when the designer approves a change. Each phase's build spec draws on it.
